    The lander

    Meet the lander.

    Fly it with the keypad (or the arrow keys and space), let the autopilot land it, refuel it from the cart, and switch on See-through to watch the fuel flow. Drag it to turn it all the way round.

    How a lander lands

    1. Gravity pulls it down. The lower it gets, the faster it falls.
    2. The engine pushes up. Helium squeezes fuel and oxidizer into it; they burn the moment they meet.
    3. Little thrusters steer. Puffs of gas nudge it left and right.
    4. Probes touch first. "Contact light!" The engine stops, and the legs' shock absorbers catch the bump.

    Thirteen worlds.

    Your best score on each world next to the AI's, kept in this browser. 200 points is a good landing; the AI usually scores 250 to 290.

    How the AI learned

    Nobody told it how to fly.

    The AI is a small neural network: 8 numbers go in (how high, how fast, how tilted…) and it picks one of 4 engines, 50 times a second. It learned the way you learn to ride a bike.

    01

    Try

    At first it presses buttons at random, wobbles, and crashes. Its first few thousand landings are all crashes.

    Practice: 26 simulators fly at once, two on every world. Each choice keeps a little randomness, so it keeps stumbling onto new moves.

    02

    Score

    After every move it gets points, like a game: closer, slower and upright is good; wasting fuel or crashing is bad.

    A reward function scores each of 50 frames a second, with potential-based shaping, so it is never rewarded for just hovering near the pad.

    03

    Improve

    It does a little more of whatever earned points, again and again. After 11.5 million tries it lands nearly every time, on every world.

    PPO nudges the network toward moves that scored better than it expected; the Universal pilot first studied each world's best pilot, then all 13 at once.

    Mission control

    Go further.

    PerfectLand's mission control trains pilots on your computer while you watch: live charts of every attempt, a 3D flight deck, and a look inside the network as it decides. One network can learn every world at once.
